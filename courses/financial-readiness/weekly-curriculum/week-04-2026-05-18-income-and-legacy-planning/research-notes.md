# Week 4 Research Notes - Managing Retirement Income

Prepared: May 8, 2026  
Scope: Module 4 Week 4 materials for VUB Financial Readiness

## Research Question

What current federal, state, and consumer-finance evidence should guide Week 4 instruction on retirement income stacking, RMDs, state tax impact, and debt management for older veterans in West Virginia?

## Key Findings

### Systems Framing

Week 4 should explain veteran retirement finance as an interaction among payment systems and measurement systems. DFAS, VA, SSA, and plan custodians send money or administer accounts. IRS, Medicare/SSA, state tax agencies, and lenders measure income using different definitions and thresholds. This distinction helps students understand why a household can feel financially stable in cash-flow terms while still creating tax, Medicare premium, or debt-to-income consequences.

The most teachable control point is the traditional retirement-account withdrawal. A traditional TSP or IRA withdrawal generally raises federal adjusted gross income, which can increase the taxable share of Social Security benefits under the combined-income formula. Medicare uses modified adjusted gross income from the IRS tax return two years prior to determine whether Part B and Part D income-related monthly adjustment amounts apply. This means a one-time withdrawal or Roth conversion can have a delayed premium consequence.

This systems frame also clarifies the contrast between tax-free sources and taxable withdrawals. VA disability compensation and CRSC are tax-free, while CRDP is taxed in the same manner as retired pay. That difference is not just a label: it affects what appears on the federal return, what raises AGI, and what students should discuss with a tax professional before year-end.

### Federal Tax Treatment

Military retired pay based on age or length of service is taxable as pension income at the federal level. VA-administered veterans' benefits, including disability compensation and pension payments for disabilities, are generally not included in taxable income. This supports the course's core "taxable vs. tax-free income stack" framing.

Social Security taxation still uses the combined-income formula: adjusted gross income + nontaxable interest + one-half of Social Security benefits. SSA's 2026 retirement publication states that single filers may owe tax on up to 50% of benefits between $25,000 and $34,000 of combined income and up to 85% above $34,000; joint filers use $32,000 to $44,000 and above $44,000. TSP and traditional IRA withdrawals can therefore increase both AGI and the taxable share of Social Security.

### RMD Rules

IRS guidance confirms that RMDs generally begin at age 73 for current retirees. SECURE 2.0 raises the RMD age to 75 in two steps over 10 years, which is the basis for teaching age 75 for those born in 1960 or later. Missed RMD amounts may trigger a 25% excise tax, reduced to 10% if corrected within two years.

The previous teaching shortcut "Roth IRA has no RMD" should be expanded. Current IRS guidance says Roth IRAs and designated Roth accounts in employer plans are not subject to lifetime RMDs for the original owner, though beneficiaries remain subject to RMD rules. Traditional TSP, 401(k), 403(b), and traditional IRA balances remain subject to RMDs.

### State Tax Impact

The state tax section needed the biggest update. Soldier for Life/MyArmyBenefits reported in January 2026 that, for tax year 2025, 9 states had no income tax, 28 fully exempted military retired pay, 13 offered partial exemptions, and D.C. fully taxed military retired pay. The old "29/7/3" table was stale and double-counted New Hampshire.

New Hampshire eliminated its interest and dividends tax for tax years beginning in 2025. California added a limited military retired pay exemption for eligible taxpayers beginning in 2025. Vermont expanded its exemption to full relief under $125,000 AGI and partial relief between $125,000 and $175,000. Georgia's exemption rises to $65,000 in tax year 2026.

West Virginia is the classroom anchor: WV exempts military retired pay from state income tax, and Social Security is fully exempt from WV income tax starting in tax year 2026. For WV veterans, the state tax discussion should focus on TSP withdrawals, civilian wages, investment income, and relocation tradeoffs.

### Debt in Retirement

Debt statistics should be presented with source context. LendingTree's 2024 credit-report sample of users ages 66-71 found 97.1% had nonmortgage debt, but that is not a nationally representative survey of all retirees. AARP's 2025 survey of adults 50+ with credit card debt found that 48% of those carrying balances owed $5,000 or more and 28% owed $10,000 or more. These figures are better classroom anchors than an uncited "average balance" number.

## Instructional Implications

Do not describe California as a fully taxing state without qualification. As of tax year 2025, it has a limited exemption for eligible taxpayers.

Do not assume a move from North Carolina to Texas saves state tax on MSG Rivera's military pension. Many states now exempt military retired pay, so the real comparison may be TSP income, civilian wages, property tax, insurance, housing, healthcare access, and family support.

Teach RMD penalties as "25%, or 10% if corrected within two years" rather than simply "25%." The harsher statement is memorable but incomplete.

Add a "control calendar" exercise before tax time: what must happen before December 31, what can be delayed, which forms will arrive in January, and which decisions could affect Medicare premiums two years later.

For WV students, explicitly say: WV does not tax military retired pay, and Social Security is fully exempt from WV income tax starting in tax year 2026. Then pivot to the broader relocation lesson: total cost of living matters more than income tax alone.
