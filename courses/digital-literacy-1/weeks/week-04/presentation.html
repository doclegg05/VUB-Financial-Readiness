Your Digital Identity

Everything the internet knows about you

Your digital identity is the picture of you that builds up online — your accounts, your posts, your photos, and the details websites collect. You manage it every time you decide what to share.

You Control This

  • What you post and share
  • Which accounts you create
  • Your privacy settings
  • Who you connect with

Smart Habits

  • Share less, not more
  • Review privacy settings yearly
  • Use a separate email for sign-ups
  • Think before you post
A Simple Rule
Before you share something online, ask: "Would I be comfortable if a stranger, my family, and a future employer all saw this?"

Protecting Your Personal Information

What is PII, and why guard it?

PII stands for Personally Identifiable Information — any detail that can be used to identify you. In the wrong hands, it can lead to identity theft or fraud.

Examples of PII

  • Social Security number
  • Date of birth
  • Home address & phone
  • Bank or VA account numbers
  • Driver's license number

How to Protect It

  • Never share it in email or text
  • Only enter it on trusted sites
  • Look for the lock icon (https)
  • Shred paper documents
⚠️ Remember
No legitimate company, bank, or the VA will ask for your full Social Security number or password in an unexpected email, text, or phone call.

Reputation & Permanence

The internet has a long memory

📌
Posts Can Last Forever Even after you delete something, others may have saved it, shared it, or taken a screenshot
🔎
People Look You Up Employers, banks, and new acquaintances may search your name online
🌟
You Build Your Reputation Kind, helpful, and honest posts shape how people see you — in a good way
The Grandkid Test
If you would not want your grandchildren — or your old unit — to read it years from now, it is better not to post it.

Legal & Ethical Behavior

Doing the right thing online

✅ Good Practice

  • Give credit for others' work
  • Respect copyright on photos & music
  • Be honest and accurate
  • Treat people with respect

❌ Avoid

  • Copying work and calling it yours
  • Sharing others' private information
  • Posting harmful or untrue claims
  • Pretending to be someone else
Simple Test
The same values that guide you in person — honesty, respect, and fairness — apply online. If it would be wrong face-to-face, it is wrong on a screen.

The Impact of Negative Messages

Words on a screen still carry weight

Online communication can turn hurtful fast — rude comments, harassment, rumors, or "trolling" (posting to provoke a reaction). It affects real people.

😟
It Affects Wellbeing Negative messages can cause stress, anxiety, anger, and hurt feelings — for the target and for bystanders
⚡
It Spreads Quickly A harsh comment can be shared widely in minutes and is hard to take back
🤝
You Set the Tone Choosing to be calm and respectful — even in disagreement — makes online spaces better for everyone

Is This Information True?

Checking the validity of what you read online

Not everything online is accurate. Scams, rumors, and "fake news" are common. Before you believe — or share — something, check it.

Ask Yourself

  • Who wrote it? Are they an expert?
  • Where is it published?
  • When — is it current?
  • Why — to inform, or to sell/scare?

Red Flags

  • No author or source listed
  • Shocking headline, urgent tone
  • Spelling and grammar errors
  • Asks for money or PII
✅ Check With a Second Source
If a claim is true, you can usually confirm it on a trusted site — a major news outlet, a .gov site like va.gov, or a well-known organization. If only one place says it, be skeptical.

Anonymity & the Power of Nonresponse

Sometimes the best reply is no reply

🎭
Online Anonymity People can hide their real identity online. This can protect privacy and free speech — but it also emboldens some to behave badly because they feel they will not be held responsible
🤫
The Value of Nonresponse Trolls and bullies want a reaction. Not replying — "do not feed the troll" — removes their reward and often makes them stop
Instead of Responding, You Can:
  • Block the person so they cannot contact you
  • Report the post to the platform
  • Save a screenshot if it is serious or threatening
  • Walk away — step back and protect your peace of mind

Common Security Threats

Know the dangers so you can avoid them

🎣
Phishing Fake emails, texts, or calls that trick you into giving up passwords or PII. They often pretend to be your bank, the VA, or a delivery company
🦠
Malware & Viruses Harmful programs that sneak onto your device through bad downloads or links and can steal information or lock your files
💸
Scams & Identity Theft Tricks to take your money or use your identity — fake prizes, "tech support" calls, or romance scams
⚠️ The Golden Rule
Slow down. If a message creates urgency or fear ("act now!"), that is a warning sign. When in doubt, do not click — contact the company directly using a number you trust.

Building a Strong Passphrase

Longer is stronger

Today's best advice: use a passphrase — several words strung together. It is long, hard to guess, and easy for you to remember.

✅ A Good Passphrase
PurpleTrout-Coffee-Sunrise7
Long (16+ characters), uses unrelated words, mixes in a capital letter and a number. Easy to picture, hard to crack.

Do

  • Make it 12+ characters (longer is better)
  • Use a different password for each account
  • Consider a password manager
  • Turn on two-factor authentication

Don't

  • Use names, birthdays, or "password123"
  • Reuse the same one everywhere
  • Write it on a sticky note on the monitor
  • Share it with anyone

Resetting a Password & Locking Your Device

Two skills everyone needs

🔑 Reset a Forgotten Password

  • On the sign-in page, click "Forgot password?"
  • Confirm your identity by email or text code
  • Create a new, strong passphrase
  • Reset right away if an account is hacked

🔒 Lock Your Screen

  • Press Windows + L to lock instantly
  • Lock it whenever you step away
  • Set it to lock automatically after a few minutes
  • Your password unlocks it again
Why Lock?
Locking your screen keeps others from seeing your email, health records, or files while you are away from your desk — at the lab, the library, or a clinic.

Clearing Your Browser Data

Cleaning up history, cookies, and saved info

Your browser remembers the sites you visit (history) and stores small files called cookies. On a shared or public computer, it is smart to clear them.

  1. Open your browser's menu
    Click the three dots (⋮) in the top-right corner
  2. Find "Clear browsing data"
    It is usually under Settings, History, or Privacy. The shortcut Ctrl+Shift+Delete opens it directly
  3. Choose what to clear
    History, cookies, and cached files. Pick a time range like "Last hour" or "All time"
  4. Click "Clear data"
    Done! Note: clearing cookies will sign you out of websites
💡 On Public Computers
Always sign out of your accounts and clear browsing data before leaving a library or shared lab computer.

Tracking & Information Stored on Your Device

Who is watching, and what is saved?

👣
Navigation Tracking Websites and advertisers use cookies to follow what you do online — which is why an item you viewed seems to "follow" you in ads. It can feel convenient, but it is also a privacy trade-off
💾
Risks of Information Stored on a Device Saved passwords, autofill details, downloaded files, and photos all live on your computer or phone. If the device is lost, stolen, or shared, that information could be exposed
Protect Stored Information
  • Lock your device with a password or PIN
  • Do not save passwords on shared computers
  • Sign out and clear data when you finish
  • Remove old files and downloads you no longer need

Private Browsing

A handy tool — with limits

Most browsers offer a private window — called Incognito in Chrome or InPrivate in Edge. Open one with Ctrl+Shift+N (Chrome) or Ctrl+Shift+P (Edge/Firefox).

✅ What It Does

  • Does not save your history on that device
  • Does not keep cookies after you close it
  • Will not autofill or save logins
  • Great on shared computers

❌ What It Does NOT Do

  • It does not make you invisible
  • Your internet provider can still see sites
  • Websites you log into still know it is you
  • It is not full anonymity or protection
⚠️ Don't Be Fooled
Private mode keeps things tidy on your own device. It does not hide you from websites, your employer's network, or your internet provider.

Staying Healthy Online

Caring for your mind and body

🧠 Mental Wellbeing

  • Take breaks from screens and news
  • Limit doom-scrolling before bed
  • Unfollow accounts that stress you
  • Connect in person, too

💺 Physical & Ergonomics

  • Screen at arm's length, top at eye level
  • Sit back in the chair, feet flat
  • Keep wrists straight while typing
  • 20-20-20: every 20 min, look 20 feet away for 20 seconds
💚 Small Changes, Big Difference
Stand up and stretch every half hour. Adjusting your chair and screen can prevent neck, back, wrist, and eye strain over time.

Knowledge Check

Try each question, then reveal the answer

1. Which of these is the strongest password?
Answer A long passphrase like PurpleTrout-Coffee-Sunrise7. Length and unrelated words make it far harder to guess than a short password or a birthday.
2. A stranger posts a rude, insulting comment just to get a reaction. What is usually the best response?
Answer Do not reply. Nonresponse removes the reaction the troll wants. Then block or report them, and save a screenshot if it is threatening.
3. True or false: a private (Incognito) window makes you completely anonymous online.
Answer False. Private mode just avoids saving history and cookies on that device. Your internet provider, the network, and sites you log into can still see you.
