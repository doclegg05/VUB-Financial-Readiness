The Desktop & Operating System Terms

The home base of your Windows computer

🖥️
Desktop The main screen you see after signing in. It holds icons, shortcuts, and your background
📊
Taskbar The bar along the bottom. It shows open programs and has the Start button and clock
🪟
Start Menu & Windows Key Press the Windows key (bottom-left of the keyboard) to open Start and find any program
Why "operating system"?
The operating system (Windows) is the master program that runs your computer and lets every other app work. The desktop and taskbar are part of it.

What a Web Browser Does

Your window to the internet

🌐

A Web Browser

A program that opens websites. Common browsers are Microsoft Edge, Google Chrome, and Mozilla Firefox. They all do the same basic job.

📍
Address Bar The box at the top where you type a website address (the URL), like va.gov. You can also type a question to search
Reading a web address
In www.va.gov, the .gov ending tells you it's an official U.S. government site. A small padlock 🔒 means the connection is secure.

Tabs, Bookmarks & History

Three tools that make browsing easier

🗂️
Tabs Open several pages in one window. Each tab is like a page in a folder — click between them at the top
⭐
Bookmarks (Favorites) Save a website so you can return without retyping it. Click the star ⭐ in the address bar to bookmark a page
🕘
History A list of pages you've visited. Press Ctrl + H to find a site you saw earlier but didn't bookmark
Tip
Bookmark the websites you use most — like va.gov or your email — so they're one click away every time.

Moving Between Sites & Programs

Navigating your "digital environment"

Within the Browser

  • Back ← returns to the last page
  • Forward → goes ahead again
  • Refresh ⟳ reloads the page
  • Home 🏠 goes to your start page

Between Programs

  • Click an icon on the taskbar
  • Press Alt + Tab to switch windows
  • A browser and a document can be open at the same time
The big idea
A "digital environment" just means a place you work — a website, an app, or your desktop. The same skills move you around all of them.

Three Ways to Connect

Wired, Wi-Fi, and cellular

🔌
Wired (Ethernet) A cable plugs your computer directly into the router. Very fast and steady — common for desktop PCs
📶
Wi-Fi A wireless signal in your home or this lab. No cables — your device connects through the air to the router
📱
Cellular (Mobile Data) The same network that carries phone calls. Phones use it for internet when away from Wi-Fi
Which is best?
At home, Wi-Fi is the most common. A wired cable is steadiest. Cellular is for when you're out and about. They all get you to the same internet.

Modem, Router & Internet Provider

The gear that brings the internet to your home

  1. Internet Service Provider (ISP)
    The company you pay for internet — such as a cable or phone company. They send the signal to your house.
  2. Modem
    A box that brings the provider's signal into your home through a wall outlet.
  3. Router
    Shares that internet with your devices — by Wi-Fi or by cable. It creates your Wi-Fi network name.
Good to know
Many homes have one box that is a modem and router combined. If the internet goes out, this box is the first place to check.

Am I Connected?

How to tell if your device is online

📶
Check the Wi-Fi Icon Look at the bottom-right of the taskbar. Curved bars 📶 mean Wi-Fi is connected. More bars = stronger signal
🌐
A Globe Icon Means Trouble If you see a globe 🌐 instead of the Wi-Fi bars, you are not connected to the internet
✅
The Real Test Open your browser and visit a website you trust. If it loads, you're online
Click to see your networks
Click the Wi-Fi icon on the taskbar to see available networks and which one you're on. Your network shows as "Connected."

When the Internet Stops Working

Simple steps to try, in order

  1. Check the Wi-Fi icon
    Make sure Wi-Fi is turned on and you're connected to the right network.
  2. Try another website
    If only one site fails, the problem is that site — not your connection.
  3. Restart your modem/router
    Unplug it, wait 30 seconds, plug it back in, and wait two minutes. This fixes most problems.
  4. Restart your computer
    A simple restart clears many small glitches.
Still not working?
If none of these help, the problem may be with your internet provider. Call them — an outage in your area is not something you can fix at home.

Search Smarter: Start With Your Need

Good searches begin with a clear question

❓
Decide What You Actually Need Before typing, ask: what am I trying to find out? The clearer your question, the better the results

Vague Search

  • "benefits"
  • "doctor"
  • "taxes"

Clear Search

  • "VA disability benefits eligibility"
  • "VA clinic near Charleston WV"
  • "free tax help for veterans"
Use a few specific words
You don't need full sentences. Three or four well-chosen words usually work best.

Good Results vs. Bad Results

Not every result is worth your time

✅ Trust These More

  • Sites ending in .gov or .edu
  • Well-known organizations
  • Recent dates on the page
  • Clear, plain information

⚠️ Be Careful With

  • Results marked "Ad" or "Sponsored"
  • Sites that ask for payment or personal info
  • Pages full of pop-ups
  • Promises that sound too good
Look before you click
Read the web address under each result. Ads usually appear first and are labeled "Ad." The most useful answer is often a little further down.

Keep Track of Where You Found It

Saving your sources saves you time later

⭐
Bookmark the Page Click the star ⭐ to save a useful page so you can find it again
📋
Copy the Web Address Click in the address bar, press Ctrl + C to copy, and paste it into a note or document
📝
Note the Basics Write down the website name and the date you visited. That's enough to find it later or share it
Why bother?
If you ever need to prove where information came from — or just look it up again — your saved source is right there.

Find a Word Fast: Ctrl + F

Search inside a page or a document

  1. Press Ctrl + F
    A small search box appears, usually in the top-right corner. This works in web browsers and in programs like Word and Adobe Reader.
  2. Type the word you want
    The page jumps to it and highlights every match.
  3. Use the arrows to move
    Click the up/down arrows (or press Enter) to jump to the next match.
Why it's so useful
Instead of reading a long page to find one fact, Ctrl + F takes you straight to it. It works the same way almost everywhere.

Copyright, Public Domain & Creative Commons

What you can and can't reuse

©️
Copyright Most photos, music, and writing online belong to whoever made them. By default, you may not copy or reuse it without permission
🆓
Public Domain Work that is free for anyone to use — often because it's very old or made by the U.S. government. No permission needed
🅭
Creative Commons The creator gives permission to reuse their work, usually if you credit them. Look for a "CC" label
Simple rule
When in doubt, give credit and link back to where you found it — or look for content clearly marked free to use.

Knowledge Check

Think it through, then reveal the answer

1. You open the taskbar and see a globe icon instead of the Wi-Fi bars. What does that tell you?

The globe icon means your device is not connected to the internet. Check that Wi-Fi is on and you've joined the correct network.

2. You want to find one specific word on a long webpage. What's the fastest way?

Press Ctrl + F, type the word, and the page jumps to it and highlights every match.

3. A photo online has no special label. Can you assume it's free to copy and reuse?

No. Most online content is protected by copyright by default. Only reuse it if it's clearly public domain, marked Creative Commons, or you have permission.
